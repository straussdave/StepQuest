\chapter{Discussion}

\section{Interpretation of the Main Findings}

The evaluation of \textit{StepQuest} produced mixed results regarding its perceived influence on walking motivation, while the findings concerning engagement with the game itself were more positive. The post-questionnaire showed that three of the seven respondents agreed that they felt more motivated to walk after using \textit{StepQuest}, whereas four disagreed. Responses to whether the daily quests encouraged participants to walk more than usual were similarly mixed. Consequently, the results do not provide clear evidence that the prototype consistently increased participants' motivation to walk.

At the same time, several findings indicate that the connection between walking and game progression was generally understood and perceived positively. All post-questionnaire respondents agreed that the relationship between real-world steps and in-game progress was clear. Most respondents also considered the step-progress feedback useful. This suggests that the central interaction concept of translating physical activity into visible game progress was successfully communicated by the prototype.

The narrative and progression systems appeared to be particularly relevant for engagement. Six of seven respondents agreed that they wanted to continue the story and recover additional parts. When asked which element had motivated them most, three participants selected story progression and three selected the collection of ship parts, while one selected the daily walking goal. These responses suggest that the game-related consequences of walking were more important to participants than the walking goal in isolation. This supports the design decision to embed step goals within a larger progression structure rather than presenting walking merely as a numerical fitness target.

This interpretation is also reflected in the usage logs. The nine available log files showed a median of 15 active days and 25 application sessions per participant. Participants completed a median of five unique quests, while four log files contained unlock events for all twelve available quests. The recorded use therefore extended beyond initial experimentation for several participants and indicates repeated engagement with the prototype over multiple days. However, these usage patterns should not be interpreted as evidence that \textit{StepQuest} caused participants to walk more. The logs describe interaction with the application and progression within the game, but no objective physical-activity baseline or control group was available for comparison.

The findings are broadly consistent with the motivational concepts considered during the design of \textit{StepQuest}. Within Octalysis, \textit{Development and Accomplishment} was represented through daily step goals, visible progress, and quest completion, while \textit{Ownership and Possession} was represented through the persistent collection of recovered ship parts. The positive responses concerning story progression additionally indicate that \textit{Epic Meaning and Calling} contributed to the experience by connecting individual walking quests to a larger narrative objective. The importance attributed to the story and collection system suggests that these motivational elements were among the stronger aspects of the implemented design.

The clear step-progress feedback can also be associated with \textit{Empowerment of Creativity and Feedback}, although the possibilities for player choice were comparatively limited in the prototype. Participants could select between available quests, but the underlying interaction remained intentionally simple. The results therefore suggest that immediate and understandable feedback may have been more relevant in this prototype than extensive player agency.

From the perspective of the Behaviour Change Wheel, the implemented mechanics primarily addressed motivation through \textit{Persuasion} and \textit{Incentivisation}. Narrative progression provided an additional reason for walking, while unlockable content acted as a reward for completing physical-activity goals. Clear goals, feedback, and simple interaction were intended to support \textit{Enablement} by reducing unnecessary barriers between walking and game progression. The study cannot determine whether these intervention functions produced measurable behavior change, but the questionnaire results suggest that the incentive and progression mechanisms were perceived positively even when their influence on walking motivation differed between participants.

An important counterpoint was the occurrence of technical difficulties. Responses to the negatively worded item concerning technical problems were divided, with three participants agreeing that technical issues made the application harder to use. Qualitative feedback particularly indicated problems with delayed or unreliable step updates. For a game in which physical activity is the primary input mechanism, such problems directly affect the core interaction. If users cannot reliably observe the relationship between their walking and subsequent game progress, the motivational effect of goals and rewards may be weakened. Improving the reliability and transparency of step tracking is therefore likely to be more important than introducing additional game mechanics in a future version.

The one-quest-per-day restriction also produced a trade-off. It was designed to distribute progression across multiple days and thereby encourage repeated interaction rather than allowing the complete prototype to be finished in a single session. This corresponds to the Octalysis concept of \textit{Scarcity and Impatience}. However, qualitative feedback indicated that the restriction could also be perceived as limiting. A future implementation may therefore benefit from retaining daily progression while allowing optional secondary activities after the main quest has been completed.


\section{Research Questions and Hypotheses}

The first research question examined the perceived influence of
\textit{StepQuest} on participants' motivation to walk:

\begin{quote}
	\textbf{RQ1:} How does the use of \textit{StepQuest} influence users'
	self-reported motivation to walk?
\end{quote}

The post-questionnaire results indicate that the perceived influence on
walking motivation differed between participants. Three of the seven
respondents agreed that they felt more motivated to walk after using
\textit{StepQuest}, while four disagreed. Responses to whether the daily
quests encouraged participants to walk more than usual were similarly
mixed, with three agreeing, two responding neutrally, and two disagreeing.
At the same time, four participants agreed that \textit{StepQuest} made
walking feel more meaningful or rewarding, while three responded neutrally.

These findings suggest that \textit{StepQuest} had a positive motivational
effect for some participants, but that this effect was not consistent
across the sample. The results therefore do not support the conclusion that
the prototype generally increased users' self-reported motivation to walk.

Accordingly, \textbf{H1}, which stated that users would report feeling more
motivated to go for walks after using \textit{StepQuest}, was
\textbf{not supported overall}. Although several participants reported a
positive influence, the responses were too mixed to support the hypothesis
for the evaluated sample.


The second research question focused on the game-design elements that
supported or limited engagement:

\begin{quote}
	\textbf{RQ2:} Which design elements of \textit{StepQuest} are perceived as
	supporting or limiting engagement with the game?
\end{quote}

The results indicate that narrative progression, collectible rewards, and
clear progress feedback were important supporting elements. Six of seven
participants agreed that they wanted to continue the story and recover
additional parts. When asked which element motivated them most, three
participants selected story progression, three selected unlocking ship
parts and the collection system, and one selected the daily walking goal.

The relationship between physical activity and game progression was also
perceived positively. All seven respondents agreed that the connection
between real-world steps and in-game progress was clear, and six considered
the step-progress feedback clear and useful. In addition, four participants
agreed that the game made walking feel more meaningful or rewarding, while
the remaining three gave neutral responses.

At the same time, technical problems represented an important limiting
factor. Responses concerning technical difficulties were divided, and the
qualitative feedback indicated problems with delayed or unreliable step
tracking. The one-quest-per-day restriction was also perceived as limiting
by some participants. These findings suggest that engagement was supported
primarily by story progression, collectible rewards, and visible progress,
while technical reliability and restrictions on continued play reduced the
experience for some users.

Based on these findings, \textbf{H2}, which stated that users would perceive
a meaningful connection between real-world walking and in-game progress,
was \textbf{supported}. The connection between steps and game progress was
understood by all post-questionnaire respondents, while responses concerning
the meaningful or rewarding nature of walking were either positive or
neutral.


\section{Limitations and Future Work}

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations need to be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the study used a small convenience sample. Twelve consenting participants provided usable pre-questionnaire responses, seven completed the post-questionnaire, and usage logs were available for nine participants. The resulting sample is too small to support broad generalizations about the effectiveness of \textit{StepQuest}.

Second, the pre- and post-questionnaires were collected anonymously and could not be linked at the individual participant level. Consequently, changes in responses before and after the testing period could not be analysed using paired comparisons. The pre-questionnaire was therefore primarily used to characterize the sample, while the post-questionnaire was used to evaluate participants' experiences and perceived influence of the prototype.

Third, the study did not include a control group. Differences in walking behavior during the testing period therefore cannot be attributed to the use of \textit{StepQuest}. Factors such as participants' normal routines, weather, work schedules, or other forms of physical activity may have influenced walking independently of the application.

Physical activity was also not measured using a separate objective baseline. Although the application recorded step-related information, inconsistencies in the logging system meant that these values could not reliably represent participants' total walking activity. Step-related log metrics were therefore not used to evaluate changes in physical activity. The analysis instead focused on application usage and game progression.

The application logs themselves contained technical irregularities. In particular, duplicate quest-completion events were generated in several files. Quest completion therefore had to be reconstructed using unique content-unlock events instead of the automatically generated completion metric. Furthermore, one log did not contain the beginning of the corresponding participant's playthrough, limiting the interpretation of total progression for that file.

Technical limitations of the prototype may also have influenced the evaluation. Some participants reported unreliable or delayed step tracking. Because step detection represents the connection between real-world activity and game progress, failures in this component could directly reduce both usability and motivation.

Finally, the prototype contained a relatively limited amount of content. Twelve quests were available, and four participants represented in the usage logs completed all available quests. The evaluation therefore provides information about short-term and medium-term interaction with the prototype, but not about whether the concept could sustain engagement over several months.

\subsection{Future Work}

Future development should first address the reliability of step tracking. Step synchronization should behave consistently when the application is running in the foreground, suspended, or reopened after an extended period. Additional feedback could also communicate when step information was last synchronized, reducing uncertainty about whether walking activity has been detected.

The progression system could be expanded with additional quests, narrative events, and collectible content. Since story progression and collection were among the most positively received elements, extending these systems represents a promising direction for maintaining engagement beyond the limited duration of the prototype.

The daily quest system could additionally be made more flexible. Instead of completely restricting users after one completed quest, the main daily quest could remain the primary progression mechanism while optional activities remain available. Such activities could provide additional interaction without allowing players to progress through the complete narrative in a single day.

Social features could also be explored in future versions. Elements such as cooperative goals, optional achievement sharing, or comparison with friends could introduce aspects of \textit{Social Influence and Relatedness}, which were largely absent from the evaluated prototype. However, such mechanics should remain optional to avoid creating unwanted competitive pressure.

A larger future evaluation should include more participants and, where feasible, a control or comparison condition. Objective physical-activity measurements collected before and during the intervention would allow changes in walking behavior to be examined more reliably. Participants should also receive pseudonymous identifiers that allow questionnaire responses and usage metrics to be linked without collecting direct personal identifiers. This would enable paired analysis of individual changes while retaining an appropriate level of participant privacy.

A longer testing period would additionally make it possible to investigate whether the observed repeated engagement persists after the novelty of the application decreases. Such a study could provide stronger evidence regarding the potential of narrative-driven exergames such as \textit{StepQuest} to support sustained everyday walking behavior.
